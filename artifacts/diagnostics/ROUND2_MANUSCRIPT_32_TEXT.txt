Airline Recovery under Delayed Information:
Residual-Risk Control
Zhangliang Chen
July 2026
Abstract
1 Introduction
Airline disruption recovery is a rolling decision problem in which operational information changes
while recovery choices are being made. The same observed delay can imply different recovery needs
because its meaning depends on the current operating state. That state also reflects recent operational
evolution, aircraft continuity, turnaround flexibility, airport conditions, downstream exposure, and
remaining execution opportunities [Hassan et al., 2021, Evler et al., 2022]. A recovery decision therefore
cannot be formed from the latest delay observation alone. It depends on how evolving information
forms the current state and how the dependencies represented by that state are preserved downstream.
This requirement extends across modeling stages. Earlier information can remain relevant after new
observations arrive because the current state is history-conditioned rather than a single snapshot.
Unresolved operating events are also dependent: an upstream realization can change downstream
departure and movement conditions. At the same time, some current information, such as timing,
identity, resource, or execution information, may remain directly relevant when the action set is formed
or actions are compared. A rolling recovery methodology must therefore distinguish information
retained through the state from information that remains directly available downstream. It must also
keep subsequent consequence and action evaluations tied to the same operating realization.
Existing research provides strong foundations for dynamic and uncertainty-aware airline recovery.
Rolling-horizon methods update recovery plans as new disruption information becomes available [Vink
et al., 2020]. Stochastic formulations account for uncertain disruption duration and for the timing of
information revelation [Zhao et al., 2023]. Predictive information has also been connected to down-
stream airline planning and recovery decisions [Lee et al., 2020, Birolini and Jacquillat, 2023]. In-
dustrial decision-support systems integrate heterogeneous operational data with large-scale recovery
solvers [Davies et al., 2026]. Information updating, uncertainty representation, predictive–prescriptive
linkage, and operational data integration are therefore established capabilities.
A remaining methodological question concerns how these functions are connected within one rolling
decision process. Historical information may be needed to form the current state, while current
operational information may still be needed to instantiate or compare recovery actions. If each function
reconstructs only its local inputs, decision-relevant information can be omitted and dependencies
established during state formation can be weakened downstream. The issue is therefore not whether
information can be updated. It is how its different decision roles and the resulting state dependence
should be organized across the recovery chain.
This study addresses two questions. First, which decision-relevant information and state dependencies
need to be retained as evolving operational information is converted into a recovery state? Second,
how should retained information and the resulting state be combined with a recovery-action space

The contributions of this paper are threefold:
•We specify the cross-stage information roles and state-dependence requirements of a rolling airline-
recovery decision chain. In particular, we distinguish information retained through the operating
state from current information that remains directly relevant to action formation and comparison.
•We formalize a decision-chain structure in which admissible history forms a recovery state, while
downstream consequence and action evaluation preserve the identity of the history-conditioned
operating realization. The same structure defines an extensible action interface: a recovery action
can enter the common evaluation space once its decision-time conditions and its transformation
of the shared consequence representation are specified. This connects information sharing, state
dependence, and action-space extension without treating all decision-relevant information as a
single state vector.
•We operationalize the decision chain as a computable airline-recovery methodology that updates
the operating state as information evolves, instantiates recovery actions from current information,
and evaluates instantiated actions on the same scenario-aligned consequence and risk basis. Newly
specified actions can enter through the same declared interface once their action contracts are
defined.
The remainder of the paper reviews the related literature, formalizes the decision chain, specifies its
empirical realization, and evaluates its methodological and operational performance.
2 Related Literature
2.1 Operational Information Sharing and the Shared Operating Picture
Information sharing is a core operational principle in collaborative aviation decision making. Airport
Collaborative Decision Making (A-CDM) requires airport operators, aircraft operators, ground han-
dlers, air navigation service providers, and the Network Manager to exchange accurate and timely
information so that decisions are based on a more consistent operating picture [EUROCONTROL,
2025, Okwir et al., 2017]. Successive turnaround milestones also link inbound and outbound opera-
tions. As a milestone changes, downstream estimates, alerts, and notifications can be revised accord-
ingly [EUROCONTROL, 2025]. Information sharing therefore affects both what operators know and
when downstream expectations are updated.
Airline disruption recovery extends this coordination problem beyond airport milestones. Recovery
decisions couple aircraft rotations, passengers, crew, maintenance, airport capacity, and other oper-
ational resources [Clausen et al., 2010, Hassan et al., 2021, Su et al., 2021, Wu et al., 2025]. Recent
work also coordinates irregular-flight recovery using information exchanged between airlines and the
Network Manager [Wu et al., 2026]. Information sharing is thus well established in both operational
practice and disruption-recovery research.

In the remainder of this paper,cross-stage information sharingrefers to the retention and reuse
of decision-relevant information across computational stages of one recovery process, rather than
to information exchange between operational stakeholders. This distinction separates the present
methodological question from the inter-organizational coordination problem addressed by A-CDM.
The unresolved issue is narrower than information availability. A shared operating picture does not
specify how accumulated information should retain its decision role after it enters a recovery model.
Historical observations may be needed to form the current recovery state, whereas current timing,
identity, resource, or execution information may remain directly relevant to action formation and
comparison. A rolling recovery methodology must therefore organize these different information roles
across successive decision representations.
2.2 Distributional Representation and Decision-Relevant Information
Probabilistic models can preserve decision-relevant uncertainty that a point estimate would suppress.
Distributional flight-delay models represent conditional uncertainty in arrival or departure outcomes
rather than reducing an unresolved operation to a single expected value [Zoutendijk and Mitici, 2021,
Dalmau et al., 2024]. This distinction matters when recovery consequences depend on threshold cross-
ings, such as exhausted turnaround buffers, missed connection margins, or closed execution windows.
Two operating states with similar expected delay can therefore create different recovery exposure.
The value of a probabilistic representation extends beyond forecast accuracy. Probabilistic delay
predictions have been passed directly to flight-to-gate assignment, while probabilistic estimates of
future airport disruptions have been incorporated into dynamic stochastic airline recovery [Zoutendijk
and Mitici, 2021, Lee et al., 2020]. Predictive–prescriptive studies similarly embed data-driven delay or
uncertainty estimates in aircraft-routing and scheduling decisions [Birolini and Jacquillat, 2023, Pang
et al., 2024]. These studies show that probabilistic output can preserve uncertainty for downstream
operational use rather than collapsing it before the decision stage.
Preserving uncertainty is not the same as preserving state dependence. Separate marginal distributions
can retain uncertainty for individual quantities while discarding dependence among them. Bayesian
delay-propagation models explicitly represent dependencies among operational variables, and rela-
tional dynamic Bayesian networks propagate uncertainty across disruption-management states [Wu
and Law, 2019, Ogunsina et al., 2022]. When several unresolved quantities jointly define an operating
state, their joint or scenario-level structure must remain identifiable for state-consistent downstream
evaluation. Distributional representation can therefore support cross-stage information sharing, but
its decision value depends on how the represented operating realizations are carried forward.
2.3 State Dependence in Dynamic and Integrated Recovery
Dynamic and stochastic recovery has established that airline recovery decisions depend on an evolving
operating state. Rolling-horizon methods recompute aircraft-recovery solutions as new disruptions
occur [Vink et al., 2020]. Two-stage and scenario-based formulations account for uncertain disruption
duration and the timing of improved disruption information [Zhao et al., 2023]. Dynamic stochastic
recovery also combines realized disruptions with probabilistic information about future airport condi-
tions [Lee et al., 2020]. These studies provide a strong basis for repeated decisions under progressively
revealed information.
Operational dependencies make this state dependence consequential. Disturbances propagate through
aircraft rotations and connected airport processes, while turnaround-aware recovery shows that the
consequence of a delay depends on local flexibility and downstream conditions [Fleurquin et al., 2013,
Evler et al., 2022]. Integrated recovery further couples aircraft, crew, passenger, and schedule decisions.
The feasibility and value of an action therefore depend on the recovery instance in which it is applied
[Arikan et al., 2016, Maher, 2016, Ding et al., 2023]. Reinforcement-learning formulations express the
same idea by conditioning recovery actions on a current disruption state [Ding et al., 2023].

The gap is not a lack of state dependence in prior models. Existing formulations preserve the depen-
dencies required within their own forecasting, propagation, stochastic-optimization, or state–action
structures. The less explicit issue arises when separate modeling functions contribute sequentially to
one rolling decision. A history-conditioned operating realization may be represented probabilistically,
translated into consequences, and then modified by candidate recovery actions. Cross-stage consis-
tency therefore requires that these transformations continue to refer to the same underlying operating
realization.
2.4 From Information Integration to Decision-Chain Consistency
Integrated recovery and industrial decision support demonstrate that heterogeneous information, re-
sources, and recovery actions can be combined at operational scale. Airline-recovery formulations al-
ready cover retiming, cancellation, aircraft and crew rerouting, passenger reaccommodation, departure
holding, and cruise-speed control [Arikan et al., 2016, Maher, 2016, Ding et al., 2023]. The Lufthansa
Group decision-support system integrates aircraft, crew, maintenance, passenger, and schedule infor-
mation for large-scale operational recovery [Davies et al., 2026]. Collaborative irregular-flight recovery
has also been extended to coordinated airline–Network Manager decisions [Wu et al., 2026].
These advances shift the methodological question from integration to interface consistency. Infor-
mation availability does not determine which information should be retained through a history-
conditioned state and which should remain directly reusable downstream. Probabilistic prediction
can preserve uncertainty, but it does not ensure that later consequence models refer to the same oper-
ating realization. Integrated recovery can compare broad action sets, but a larger action domain does
not itself define how a new action should enter a common state-dependent consequence representation.
The remaining gap is therefore a decision-chain consistency problem. A rolling recovery methodology
must specify how evolving information forms the recovery state, how decision-relevant information
remains available across later stages, and how the identity of a history-conditioned operating realization
is preserved as it becomes a baseline and then an action-conditioned consequence. The same interface
should also admit additional recovery actions without requiring the upstream state to be redefined.
Section 3 formalizes these cross-stage information-sharing and state-dependence requirements.
3 Cross-Stage Information Sharing and State-Dependent Recovery
Rolling airline recovery uses information in two ways. Admissible history forms the current recovery
state, while current information can remain directly relevant when recovery actions are instantiated
and compared. The state formed upstream must also remain coherent as it is translated into conse-
quences and modified by recovery actions. The methodology therefore separates information retained
through the recovery state from information reused directly downstream. This separation also provides
a common interface through which the recovery-action library can be extended without redefining the
upstream state construction.
In this study, state dependence means that downstream recovery representations are formed as trans-
formations of the same history-conditioned operating realization rather than being reconstructed in-
dependently at each stage. The same unresolved operating realization is therefore retained through
consequence formation and action evaluation.

3.6 Rolling Updating, Information Sharing, and State Dependence
At a later decision nodet ′ > t, newly available information updatesE i,t′. The admissible history
and recovery state may change. New timing, resource, or execution information can also change the
episode-specific action space. The resultingD i,t′ is recomputed from information available att ′; later
observations do not retrospectively alter the state used for an earlier decision.
The rolling decision chain is governed by two cross-stage requirements. The first isinformation
sharing: decision-relevant information is either retained through the history-conditioned recovery state
or reused directly when current actionability and comparison conditions are formed. The second is
state dependence: downstream consequences and action-conditioned outcomes remain transformations
of the same unresolved operating realization rather than independently reconstructed local states. In

The separation of state construction from action contracts also makes the action interface extensible.

The two requirements also explain the ordering of the decision chain. Decision-time information must
be established before the recovery state can be formed. The unresolved operating realization must
be represented before state-contingent consequences can be constructed. A common baseline conse-
quence must exist before alternative recovery actions can be evaluated. Supported action-conditioned
consequences must then be aligned before formal comparison. In compact form,
information→operating state→consequence→intervention→comparison.
The ordering constrains the required transformations; it does not require a unique estimator or software
architecture. Section 4 gives the empirical realization used in this study.
4 Empirical Realization of the Decision Chain
Section 3 defines the recovery decision chain independently of a particular dataset or estimator. This
section provides one Data2 realization of its two primary mappings and the parallel action-instantiation
branch. A common decision-time information set is first constructed from the available operational
records. Its admissible history forms the recovery state, while current information is reused in par-
allel to instantiate the episode-specific action space. The two branches then rejoin when candidate
actions are evaluated against the same operating realization and aligned consequence representation.
Detailed source contracts, valuation parameters, action-response specifications, and software lineage
are reported in the Appendix.
